🔍 Reading the number critically

Data quality matters. My first attempt gave 6.1 t. Re-entering my household (4 of us, not our tenants) and our electricity more carefully gave 5.8 t, mostly through Energy (0.5 → 0.3 t) and Office (0.4 → 0.2 t).

The tool is Indian. It assumes coal-heavy grid power (≈0.7 kg CO₂/kWh). Nepal’s grid is mostly hydropower (≈0.02–0.03 kg across its life cycle, plus some dry-season imports). And Food at 2.5 t looks high for my modest diet: my own estimate from Poore & Nemecek (2018) factors is closer to ≈1.2 t, suggesting the calculator treats any non-veg diet coarsely.

🧭 What this told me about my lifestyle

I ride the bus, compost and heat water with the sun, yet my number sits above the global average. Food and Household make up ≈78% of my footprint, while transport is just 12%. The choices I never thought of as “climate decisions” matter most: mutton or dal, rice or dhido, LPG or induction, a new gadget or none.

The calculator’s own advice agreed: “Reduce meat consumption by 1–2 days per week” (High priority) and “share resources among the people living in the household”.

Step 2 Choose my challenge

Five changes, aimed at the biggest slices

I didn’t pick generic tips. I started from what I already do right, then aimed each change at what’s left: my plate, my kitchen, my shopping and our water. Each one fits my real week (office Sunday–Friday, family meals at home).

Method notes & emission factors
  • Mutton meal swapped: ≈4.8 kg CO₂e (120 g mutton × 39.7 kg CO₂e/kg, minus a pulse curry). Chicken meal: ≈1.2 kg (120 g × 9.9). Global medians from Poore & Nemecek (2018).
  • Dhido/roti instead of rice: ≈0.43 kg per dinner (150 g rice at 4.45 kg CO₂e/kg vs millet/wheat at ≈1.6). Paddy methane drives the difference.
  • Induction instead of LPG: assumes our family uses ≈1 cylinder every 6 weeks (≈92 kg CO₂ per person a year, at 2.98 kg CO₂/kg LPG). Moving cooking to induction saves ≈80% of that on Nepal’s mostly-hydro grid.
  • New item not bought: ≈10 kg CO₂e each, a conservative average for a clothing item or small online order (a laptop is ≈200–350 kg).
  • Water: shown in litres and tankers (≈10,000 L each) rather than CO₂, because its main impact is on groundwater, not the atmosphere.

Step 4 Think beyond carbon

My ecological footprint is bigger than my carbon number

A carbon footprint counts gases. An ecological footprint counts the land and sea area — cropland, grazing land, forests, fishing grounds, built-up land — needed to supply what I consume and absorb what I waste (Wackernagel & Rees, 1996). Two of my everyday choices connect directly to it:

1.7

Earths — roughly how many humanity currently uses each year, by Global Footprint Network estimates. We are drawing down nature’s capital, not living on its interest.
